The largest city on Earth

Six thousand years of the world's biggest cities, from Uruk to Dhaka. Which city was the largest at each moment, how big it was, and how the centre of the urban world wandered from Mesopotamia to China, out to Europe and back to Asia.

Around 2800 BC the largest city in the world was Uruk, in the marshy south of what is now Iraq, with perhaps {{reign.Uruk.peak|int}} people. In 2025 it is Jakarta, with {{un.jakarta2025|m1}} million. In between, the title passed through the cities of Sumer and Egypt, Babylon, Alexandria and Rome, Constantinople, Chang'an and Baghdad, the capitals of Song and Ming China, London, New York and Tokyo. By the United Nations' latest projections it will belong to Dhaka by 2050.

Nobody counted most of these people. The figures before the twentieth century are the estimates of two historians who spent years on them: Tertius Chandler, whose Four Thousand Years of Urban Growth (1987) gathered city sizes from censuses, travellers and earlier scholars, and George Modelski, whose World Cities (2003) did the same for the ancient world. In 2016 Meredith Reba, Femke Reitsma and Karen Seto turned both books into one geocoded dataset. After cleaning it holds {{source.places|int}} places and {{source.estimates|int}} population estimates. From 1975 I switch to the UN's World Urbanization Prospects 2025, which counts cities in a new way, explained below. Read every figure before 1950 as an informed estimate, and often a round one.

    02 · The crown{{n_crown_sole|int}} cities, six thousand years

    Modelski's list starts in 3700 BC with a single town, Eridu, at about {{S.-3700.pop|int}} people. By 3500 BC its neighbour Uruk had overtaken it, and Uruk stayed the largest city in every snapshot to {{reign.Uruk.last|yr}}, when Modelski puts it at {{reign.Uruk.peak|int}}. Then the crown moved around the city states of Sumer, Lagash, Mari and Girsu, to Ur, which in {{size.first_100k.y|yr}} became the first city of {{size.first_100k.pop|int}} in Modelski's list. In 2000 BC Chandler puts Memphis, Egypt's old capital, at {{S.-2000.pop|int}}; he gives Ur {{txt.ur2000_c|int}}, but Modelski gives it only {{txt.ur2000_m|int}}, so on the average Memphis comes out on top. Babylon led in {{reign.Babylon.first|yr}}. Egyptian capitals, Avaris, Thebes and Pi-Ramesses, led in {{txt.egypt_n|int}} of the {{txt.egypt_of|int}} snapshots from 1600 to 1000 BC; the exception is 1300 BC, when Yin, the last capital of China's Shang kings, had {{S.-1300.pop|int}}.

    How big the biggest city was, and which city it was

    The largest population at each snapshot (log scale), with the bar below coloured by the region of the city on top. Dashed lines: the largest figure in each compilation on its own. The time axis is stretched towards the present. Hover or tap for each snapshot.

    From 1000 BC Modelski lists only cities of at least 100,000, and for several centuries his top cities sit exactly on that line, so Babylon, Memphis and Thebes tie in 900 and 800 BC and {{txt.tie700_n|int}} cities tie in 700 BC. The classical world brought the first giants. Linzi, capital of the state of Qi in eastern China, led in 500 and 300 BC; Alexandria in 200 and 100 BC, when Modelski gives it {{peak.alexandria.m_peak|int}}, the first city of a million in his list; and then Rome, the largest city in every snapshot from AD 1 to AD {{reign.Rome.last|raw}}. Modelski puts Rome at {{peak.rome.m_peak|int}} in AD {{peak.rome.m_peak_y|raw}}; Chandler's highest figure for ancient Rome is {{peak.rome.c_peak1000|int}}.

    After Rome came Constantinople (AD 500 and 600), Ctesiphon, the Persian capital on the Tigris (622, in Chandler's figures), Chang'an, the Tang capital now called Xi'an (700), and Baghdad, which ties with Chang'an in 800 and leads alone in 900 and 1000. Chandler's own peak for Baghdad is {{baghdad_peak_c.pop|int}} in AD {{baghdad_peak_c.y|raw}}. From 1100 only Chandler's figures remain, and they keep the crown in China for most of the next seven centuries: Kaifeng, the Song capital ({{S.1100.pop|int}} in 1100), briefly Merv in Central Asia (1150), Hangzhou, the southern Song capital, from 1200 to 1350 (peaking at {{S.1300.pop|int}}), Nanjing in 1400, then Beijing from 1450 to 1600 and again from 1750 to 1825. In between, Ottoman Istanbul led in 1650 and 1700 with {{S.1700.pop|int}}.

    London took over in the middle of the nineteenth century, New York in the twentieth, and Tokyo by 1970. In the UN's figures Tokyo stays on top until Jakarta draws level in 2010 and passes it; Jakarta is then the largest in every snapshot to 2045, and Dhaka edges past it in {{un.dhaka_first|raw}}.

    From Kaifeng in 1100 to Beijing in 1825, the largest city in the world was Chinese in {{txt.china_n|int}} of {{txt.china_of|int}} snapshots, and until 1800 it never held more than {{size.max_1100_1750|int}} people.

    03 · SizeFrom tens of thousands to tens of millions

    The biggest city grew slowly for most of history and then very fast. Uruk's {{reign.Uruk.peak|int}} of 2800 BC was not beaten until {{txt.uruk_passed_by|raw}} in {{txt.uruk_passed_y|yr}}, {{txt.uruk_passed_years|int}} years later. The largest city first reached 100,000 in {{size.first_100k.y|yr}} (Ur, in Modelski's list) and a million in {{size.first_1m.y|yr}} (Alexandria, also Modelski). Chandler is more cautious: his first city over 100,000 is Avaris in {{size.first_100k_c.y|yr}}, and at the snapshot dates his first million city is Beijing in {{size.first_1m_c.y|raw}}, with {{size.first_1m_c.pop|int}}. Between 1100 and 1750 the largest city in the combined estimates never passed {{size.max_1100_1750|int}}.

    Industrial London broke the pattern: {{size.y1900|int}} people in 1900. New York was the first city past ten million in these figures ({{size.first_10m.pop|int}} in 1950) and Tokyo the first past twenty million ({{size.first_20m.pop|int}} in Chandler's 1970 figure). In the UN's count Tokyo had {{size.y1975|int}} in 1975, Jakarta has {{size.y2025|int}} in 2025, and Dhaka is projected to reach {{size.y2050|int}} by 2050. The largest city of 2025 is {{size.x1800_2025|f1}} times the size of the largest in 1800.

    04 · WhereThe wandering centre of the urban world

    To see where the big cities were, I took the {{source.topn|int}} largest places at each snapshot and found their centre, weighted by population. Until 800 BC it never left the band between {{txt.meso_lon_min|int}}°E and {{txt.meso_lon_max|int}}°E, from the Nile to the edge of Iran: it was at {{cog_sum.c_m3000.lat|f1}}°N {{cog_sum.c_m3000.lon|f1}}°E in 3000 BC, close to Uruk. As Chinese and Indian cities joined the list in the first millennium BC it moved east into Central Asia, reaching {{cog_sum.c_m500.lon|int}}°E by 500 BC, and Rome then pulled it back west to {{cog_sum.c100.lon|int}}°E in AD 100. From AD 500 to 1825 it stayed between {{txt.lon500_1825_min|int}}°E and {{txt.lon500_1825_max|int}}°E, over Central Asia, poised between the Islamic world, India and China: {{cog_sum.c1500.lon|int}}°E in 1500 and {{cog_sum.c1800.lon|int}}°E in 1800.

    Where the {{source.topn|int}} largest cities were

    Share of the population of the {{source.topn|int}} largest places at each snapshot, by region (fewer places before about 500 BC, when the lists are short). Hover or tap.

    Then it lurched west. In the nineteenth century Europe's industrial cities and then America's took over the list: by 1900 {{reg_sum.eu_1900_n|int}} of the {{source.topn|int}} largest cities were European, holding {{reg_sum.eu_1900|int}}% of their people, and the centre sat at {{cog_sum.c1900.lat|int}}°N {{txt.c1900_lon|f1}}°{{txt.c1900_hem|raw}}, north of Scotland, {{cog_sum.km_1800_1900|int}} km from where it had been a century before. Its westernmost point came in {{cog_sum.west_y|raw}}, between Iceland and Greenland. After 1975 it raced back east and south: by 2025 it was at {{cog_sum.c2025.lat|int}}°N {{cog_sum.c2025.lon|int}}°E, in eastern Tibet, and by 2050 it is projected at {{cog_sum.c2050.lat|int}}°N {{cog_sum.c2050.lon|int}}°E, in eastern Nepal. In 2025, {{reg_sum.asia_2025_n|int}} of the {{source.topn|int}} largest cities are in Asia and {{reg_sum.eu_2025_n|int}} is in Europe.

    The centre of the {{source.topn|int}} largest cities, east and west

    Longitude of the population-weighted centre of the {{source.topn|int}} largest places (solid) and of the 10 largest (thin), at each snapshot. Hover or tap.

    Two cautions. A centre found this way is the average of points on a globe, so when the big cities are spread across Europe, America and Asia it falls far to the north, between them: in {{cog_sum.west_y|raw}} it is at {{cog_sum.west.lat|int}}°N. And part of the jump between 1970 and 1975 is the change of source, because the UN's definition makes Asian cities larger and American ones smaller (section 07).

    05 · LondonLondon's century

    London was {{txt.london_rank1600_ord|raw}} in Chandler's list in 1600, with {{london.p1600|int}} people, {{txt.london_rank1700_ord|raw}} in 1700 and {{txt.london_rank1750_ord|raw}} in 1750. In 1800 it was second only to Beijing ({{london.p1800|int}} against {{london.bj1800|int}}), and in 1825 it was {{london.gap1825_pct|f1}}% behind ({{london.p1825|int}} against {{london.bj1825|int}}), well within the uncertainty of either figure. By 1850 it was clearly the largest city in the world, with {{london.p1850|int}}, {{london.x1850|f2}} times Beijing.

    06 · UncertaintyTwo compilations, one crown

    Chandler and Modelski often disagree, and before AD 1000 the choice of compilation can change the answer. At the {{n_both|int}} snapshots where both have figures for at least {{method.min_places|int}} places, they name a different largest city at {{n_disagree|int}}. The starkest case is AD 1000: Modelski gives Baghdad {{baghdad1000.m|int}}, Chandler gives it {{baghdad1000.c|int}} and makes {{baghdad1000.c_lead|raw}} in Spain the largest city, with {{baghdad1000.c_lead_pop|int}}. Averaged, Baghdad still leads with {{baghdad1000.comb|int}}. Chandler's highest figure for ancient Rome is {{txt.rome_c_share|int}}% of Modelski's, and for Alexandria {{txt.alex_c_share|int}}%.

    Most snapshots before 1000 BC rest on Modelski alone, and many later ones on Chandler alone. Some estimates are interpolated: where Chandler has no figure for a place at a snapshot date, I draw a straight line on a log scale between his two nearest figures, if they are close enough in time; {{interp.top10_pct|f1}}% of the top ten places at the historical snapshots involve such a step. Reba and colleagues also gave each place a certainty score from 1 (most certain) to 3 for how securely it was identified and placed: {{cert.lead_c1|int}} of the {{cert.lead_n|int}} crown holders before 1975 score 1, and the exceptions are {{txt.cert_list|raw}}.

    07 · DefinitionsWhat counts as a city? The 1975 seam

    The UN's World Urbanization Prospects used to rely on each country's own idea of a city or urban agglomeration, which made comparisons awkward. The 2025 revision instead applies the Degree of Urbanisation, a method agreed by the UN Statistical Commission: a city is a cluster of contiguous 1 km grid cells with at least {{method.degurba_density|int}} people per square kilometre and at least {{method.degurba_min|int}} people in all, found in satellite-based population grids. It ignores administrative borders and asks only where people live densely. That changes the familiar rankings. Jakarta and Dhaka come out larger than in older lists, because their dense built-up areas sprawl across many districts; American cities come out smaller, because their suburbs are not dense enough to count; and some densely settled countrysides become very large "cities", such as Hajipur in Bihar, India, with {{un.hajipur.p2025|int}} people in 2025.

    The seam shows clearly in 1975. Chandler gives Tokyo {{seam_sum.tokyo_ch|int}} and the UN {{seam_sum.tokyo_un|int}}, close enough. But Chandler's New York, {{seam_sum.ny_ch|int}}, becomes {{seam_sum.ny_un|int}} under the UN's definition, and Jakarta grows from {{seam_sum.jakarta_ch|int}} ({{txt.jakarta_ch_rank_ord|raw}} in Chandler's list) to {{seam_sum.jakarta_un|int}} ({{txt.jakarta_un_rank_ord|raw}}). Chandler's 1975 list counts {{n.ch1975_m1|int}} places of a million or more and the UN {{n.m1_1975|int}}; Modelski's list of million cities in 2000 has {{n.mod2000_m1|int}}, the UN {{n.un2000_m1|int}}. I have not blended the two kinds of figure: on the map, the charts and in the text, 1975 and later is the UN, and everything before is Chandler and Modelski.

    08 · 2050Who rises next

    The UN projects every city forward to 2050. In its figures Jakarta passed Tokyo in {{un.jakarta_first|raw}} (they are within {{source.tie_pct|f1}}% of each other in that year's snapshot), Dhaka passed Tokyo in {{un.dhaka_over_tokyo|raw}}, and Dhaka is projected to pass Jakarta in {{un.dhaka_first|raw}}, reaching {{un.dhaka2050|m1}} million to Jakarta's {{un.jakarta2050|m1}} million. That is a margin of {{un.dhaka_margin_2050_pct|f1}}%, well within the uncertainty of a projection, so the honest reading is that Dhaka and Jakarta will be the two giants of 2050. Tokyo, which peaked at {{un.tokyo_peak|m1}} million in {{un.tokyo_peak_y|raw}}, is projected to shrink to {{un.tokyo2050|m1}} million.

    09 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

    Data. The historical figures are Reba, Reitsma and Seto's (2016) digitisation of Chandler (1987) and Modelski (2003), on figshare: Chandler's compilation ({{source.chandler_rows|int}} places, {{source.chandler_values|int}} figures from {{source.chandler_first|yr}} to 1975), Modelski's ancient cities ({{source.ancient_rows|int}} places, {{source.ancient_values|int}} figures, 3700 BC to AD 1000) and Modelski's {{source.modern_rows|int}} cities of a million or more in 2000. I used the V2 files. Compared with V1 they trim stray spaces from {{source.v1_padded|int}} names and countries (leaving {{source.v2_padded|int}}), correct "Instanbul" to Istanbul, move Kiev from the Russian Federation to Ukraine, and fold V1's duplicate Guadalajara row and a misspelt "Madgdeburg" row into one row each, which is why Chandler has {{source.v2_rows|int}} rows rather than {{source.v1_rows|int}}; no figure changes. The files are Windows-1252 text, not UTF-8, and must be read as such (Košice, Šomron); {{source.lost_chars|int}} alternative names had already lost characters to question marks before the files were written. The modern figures are the UN World Urbanization Prospects 2025 files 18 to 21: the 100 largest cities at each date, the 100 largest of 2025 through time, capitals, and all {{source.un_cities|int}} cities that reach 50,000 between 1975 and 2050, each with a population-weighted centre.

    Corrections. I made {{source.fixes|int}} documented corrections, listed below. The largest is that Modelski's cities in the Americas carry, in the BC columns, exact copies of their AD figures {{method.mirror|int}} years later (Teotihuacan in 900 BC equals Teotihuacan in AD 400, and so on): I dropped those {{source.americas_copies|int}} copies. I also dropped single figures that are out by a factor of about ten from the same city's neighbours (Philadelphia in 1914 at {{txt.phil|m1}} million, for instance), figures attached to the wrong city (Memphis, Egypt, in 1975 carries the figure for Memphis, Tennessee) and two misplaced locations.

    All corrections

    Method. The snapshots are Modelski's dates up to AD 1000, plus Chandler's cross-sections in 1360, 650 and 430 BC and AD 361 and 622, then Chandler's benchmark years from 1100 to 1970, then the UN every five years. At each snapshot each compilation gives a place its own figure for that date if it has one. Otherwise Chandler's figure is interpolated on a log scale between his two nearest dates for that place if they are no more than {{method.gap1|int}} years apart before AD {{method.gap_until1|raw}}, {{method.gap2|int}} years to {{method.gap_until2|raw}} and {{method.gap3|int}} years after; Modelski's are interpolated only between neighbouring dates of his own grid, because a gap in his grid means the place was not on his list. Nothing is extrapolated. Where both compilations have a figure, I use their average. Places within {{source.tie_pct|f1}}% of the largest count as tied. I matched {{source.matched|int}} places across the two compilations by name and distance (under {{method.match_km|int}} km), and linked {{links.n|int}} historical places to the UN city that contains them, by name within {{method.link_km|int}} km. The centre of gravity averages the positions of the {{source.topn|int}} largest places as points on a sphere, weighted by population.

    Where the record is thin. The compilations are rich for Europe, the Middle East, India and China and thin elsewhere. Before AD 1000 they hold {{thin.am_pre1000|int}} places in the Americas (Modelski's are {{txt.thin_americas|raw}}) and {{thin.ssa_pre1000|int}} in sub-Saharan Africa. Between 1500 and 1799 they hold {{thin.eu_1500_1799|int}} European places, but {{thin.ea_1500_1799|int}} East Asian and {{thin.ssa_1500_1799|int}} in sub-Saharan Africa. Cahokia, on the Mississippi, has {{txt.cahokia_n|int}} figures in the whole record and Great Zimbabwe {{txt.zimbabwe_n|int}}, so the absence of a circle is not evidence that nobody lived there, and counts of cities before 1975 are floors.

    What this is not. These are estimates, often rounded to the nearest 10,000 or 100,000, and often made by working back from city walls, house counts, food supplies or later censuses; a factor of two either way is entirely possible for an ancient city. "City" means different things in different sources: a walled town and its suburbs for Chandler, a metropolitan area in some modern counts, and a dense grid cluster for the UN. The 2025 to 2050 figures are projections. And the names are modern conveniences: I give period names where the text needs them (Chang'an, Constantinople, Edo), but the map uses one name per place.
